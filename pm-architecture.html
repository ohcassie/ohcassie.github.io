Performance Management Architecture

Redesigning how performance gets defined and rated after three years of rating inflation and a full reset of enterprise priorities. From stakeholder input to a four-pillar framework.

DWG-01 · Site Survey

The curve was broken, then the priorities changed under it

Three years ago the message was simple: "shoot for a 3." On a 1–5 scale, a 3 was solid performance, and the push was mostly about getting reviews completed at all. It worked, but not the way it was supposed to: managers converged on the top of the scale, rating almost everyone a 5 rather than differentiating real performance.

Since then, a leadership change at the ELT level reset what the business actually tracks. Corporate priorities are now set multiple years out. Leaders started saying the same thing in different words: the review process takes too long, and it still doesn't measure the work that actually matters now.

A fourth pain point sat underneath the other three: the ELT sets 5 strategic initiatives, but turning those into real work further down the org depended entirely on the functional leader in between. Some leaders made that translation well; some didn't. Years of training investment aimed at closing that gap moved the needle, but not far enough.

DWG-02 · Stakeholder Input

Built from what leaders were already saying in 1x1s

Rather than design in a vacuum, the architecture started with the pain points HR business partners were already surfacing in their regular 1x1s with SLT, VP through Director. Their feedback set the frame, then the design went back out for stress-testing before anything was finalized.

01

Listen through the business partners

Met with HRBPs to pull together the recurring themes from their VP- and Director-level 1x1s, where the current process helped and where it got in the way.

02

Draft the architecture

Translated that feedback into a framework built around what actually matters to the business now, not what the old scale happened to measure.

03

Shop it, then rework it

Ran the draft through focus groups and 1x1s with leaders and individual contributors across the org, iterating on the framework as real reactions came in.

04

Bring in ERG leaders

Sought buy-in from ERG leaders specifically. They sit closest to culture and to the organization's most engaged colleagues, and their read on the framework mattered.

DWG-03 · Philosophy

The statement that anchors the architecture

Before any pillar or rating scale, the framework needed one sentence everyone could hold onto. This is that sentence.

"Performance management is where accountability becomes real. Every colleague should be able to point to what they did, whether it moved the business forward, and whether it reflected who we say we are. A rating is not a favor. It's a clear, honest answer to the question: did the work happen, and did it happen the right way."

DWG-06 · Success Metrics

What tells us it worked

The architecture only matters if it changes behavior once it's live. Three signals will show whether it's working, without needing a target number to be meaningful on day one.

01

Rating distribution. Do Meets and Doesn't Meet actually differentiate performance, or does the org drift back toward everyone clustering at the top?

02

SMART goal completion rate. Are colleagues consistently setting and completing goals that ladder to corporate priorities, or is Pillar 2 becoming a formality?

03

Manager confidence and calibration consistency. Do leaders rate similarly-performing colleagues similarly, and do they feel equipped to have the conversation, not just fill out the form?

DWG-07 · Sign-Off

Where it stands

The framework has been socialized end to end: HRBPs, SLT, ICs, and ERG leadership. It hasn't launched yet. It's staged as a 2027 initiative, timed to roll out alongside the corporate priorities it's built to measure against.
